# Exit Readiness Plan — Positioning Growth99 for a Growth Equity Investment in 12–24 Months

**Reframed pass 2; refined pass 3; expanded pass 4 (2026-08-16).** Pass 1 analyzed Growth99 from a prospective buyer's diligence perspective. Pass 2 flipped the lens to the sell side: what has to be true for Growth99 to attract a growth equity investment — later-stage growth capital, typically combined with some liquidity for existing shareholders, as distinct from a full leveraged buyout — inside a 12–24 month window. Pass 3 added a concrete bar of financial metrics to clear and resolved the ownership structure as sound rather than a liability. **Pass 4 removes an acquisition of clinical/EHR (Electronic Health Record) software from the near-term plan** — that path is deprioritized for now, not abandoned — and lays out an organic path to the same destination, with a fuller accounting of where this could go wrong. Confidence tags on individual factual claims trace back to public research; everything else here is strategic judgment built on top of that research.

## The Big Picture

Two separate work-streams have to converge for Growth99 to be an attractive growth equity candidate. The first, and by far the larger one, is simply running the business well enough that the underlying numbers tell a compelling story on their own — revenue expanding at a healthy clip, margins that look like software rather than an agency, and a customer base that sticks around. The second, smaller work-stream is packaging that story for an outside audience: getting the public-facing profile in order, understanding which categories of investor are the right fit, and having answers ready for the questions a screening process will inevitably ask. Neither work-stream can really substitute for the other — a great narrative wrapped around mediocre unit economics won't survive diligence, and excellent unit economics that nobody outside the company knows about won't generate inbound interest on its own. But the ratio matters: most of the next year's effort should go toward the operating fundamentals, with the market-facing work picked up later and treated as a comparatively contained, well-defined project once the fundamentals are in hand.

The connective tissue between "run the business well" and "package it for outside investors" is a specific, checkable bar of financial metrics. A growth equity investor's first pass at a company is less about the qualitative story and more a screen against a fairly standard profile — and a business can have a genuinely differentiated product and still fail to generate a second meeting if it doesn't clear that initial bar. Getting explicit about what that bar looks like, and building toward it deliberately rather than hoping the story carries the day, is the single highest-leverage thing to focus on over the next several quarters.

## The Metrics Bar, Spelled Out

None of the targets below are unusual for a vertical software business of Growth99's size — they read as baseline expectations for the category, not stretch goals reserved for top-decile performers. That's worth internalizing, because it means the gap between where the company is today and where it needs to be is probably closeable through operating discipline rather than a heroic transformation.

| Metric | Definition | Target | Why this specific bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth rate | Year-over-year growth in total recurring revenue | Roughly 13–17% annually, sustained | A single good quarter doesn't move a screen; investors want to see the trend hold across multiple reporting periods |
| Customer acquisition cost (CAC) and payback | CAC is the fully-loaded cost of sales and marketing spend required to land one new paying customer; payback period is how many months of that customer's revenue it takes to recover the CAC | Efficient enough to hold up under scrutiny — not best-in-class, but not a red flag either | Investors will recompute this themselves from the underlying data; it needs to survive that recalculation |
| Gross margin | Revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the service (cost of goods sold, or COGS — largely the labor cost of account managers, SEO specialists, and ad-campaign staff for a business like this), divided by revenue | Above roughly 75% | This is the single number that separates a "software company" valuation multiple from a "marketing agency" valuation multiple, because it's the clearest signal of how much of the revenue is genuinely automated versus hand-delivered |
| EBITDA margin (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization — a standard proxy for operating profitability) | Operating profit as a percentage of revenue | Roughly 10–15%, with the precise number depending on how much growth capital gets layered into the business afterward | High enough to demonstrate the business doesn't need external capital to survive, low enough that it's obviously still investing in growth rather than harvesting profit |
| Net revenue retention (NRR) and gross churn | NRR measures how much recurring revenue survives from one year to the next among existing customers, after accounting for cancellations, downgrades, and any expansion revenue from upsells — a figure above 100% means expansion revenue is outpacing losses; gross churn is simply the percentage of revenue or logos lost, ignoring any expansion | "Reasonable" relative to other small-and-medium-business (SMB) software peers, not enterprise SaaS benchmarks | Growth99's customers are mostly small, often single-location practices, which is a structurally higher-churn segment than enterprise software — the bar has to be set against the right comparison group |

The underlying dynamic that makes this achievable faster than it might sound: because Growth99 already has real scale — over a thousand practices on the platform — the business has meaningful operating leverage. A modest percentage-point improvement in either the win rate on new sales or the retention rate on existing customers doesn't just help one line item; it ripples through the recurring-revenue base and shows up in several of the metrics above simultaneously. Practically, that means the team shouldn't think of these five targets as five independent projects requiring five separate initiatives — a well-chosen set of two or three changes, executed well, is likely to move several of them at once.

## Path Forward Without a Near-Term Clinical Software Acquisition

Earlier drafts of this plan treated acquiring a smaller electronic medical record (EMR) or electronic health record (EHR) company — specifically Symplast, profiled elsewhere in this vault — as the centerpiece of the value-creation story: buy the clinical-charting capability Growth99 currently lacks, and use it to reposition the company from "a marketing tool" to "the system of record" for aesthetic practices. That's being set aside for now. An acquisition, even a small one, introduces integration risk, diverts management bandwidth, and very plausibly depresses the EBITDA margin target in the near term (acquired businesses rarely arrive at target margins on day one, and integration itself costs money) — all at precisely the moment when the priority is showing clean, steadily improving, organically-driven numbers. Pursuing a deal right now would be optimizing for a future storyline at the expense of the metrics bar that has to be cleared first.

The alternative path leans on four levers that don't require M&A capital or integration risk:

**1. Make the AI product's impact provable, and use it to drive both growth and margin.** The company's AI-driven lead-response tool is currently described in marketing terms — fast response times, always-on availability — but there is no independent, quantified evidence of what it actually does to conversion rates or customer retention. Closing that gap matters twice over: quantified before-and-after data (response time, lead-to-booked-appointment conversion, retention lift, ideally broken out by practice) turns a marketing claim into a genuine, defensible part of the growth story, and applying that same technology internally — to reduce the amount of manual account-management and campaign-management labor required per customer — is a direct, provable lever on the gross margin target, arguably a more convincing one than the customer-facing pitch, because it shows up in the cost structure rather than in a sales deck.

**2. Rebalance the services delivery model deliberately, not uniformly.** Gross margin above 75% likely requires reducing the labor intensity of service delivery, but doing that indiscriminately risks the opposite problem: cutting hands-on account management for accounts that value it could push churn in the wrong direction, working directly against the retention target. A more defensible approach is tiered delivery — heavier automation and self-service tooling for smaller or lower-tier accounts where the cost of white-glove service doesn't pencil out anyway, preserved or even increased high-touch service for larger accounts and at-risk renewals where the retention economics justify the labor cost. This is a segmentation exercise as much as an automation project.

**3. Treat sales efficiency and early-tenure churn as linked problems.** A meaningful share of churn in a business serving small, single-location practices likely concentrates in the first several months of the relationship — customers who were mis-sold, under-onboarded, or never saw enough value to justify the price. Tightening qualification criteria on the sales side (even at some near-term cost to gross bookings) and investing in a more deliberate onboarding motion can improve both the acquisition efficiency metric and the retention metric together, rather than treating them as separate initiatives competing for resources.

**4. Answer the clinical-charting gap with partnership rather than ownership.** The competitive research elsewhere in this vault is candid that Growth99 lacks electronic medical record and clinical-charting functionality, and that this is a real, recurring reason competitors position against it. Not acquiring that capability doesn't mean ignoring the objection — a lighter-weight technical integration or referral partnership with an existing EMR/EHR provider can address the "we don't have this" conversation without the capital outlay, execution risk, or margin drag of ownership. This keeps the acquisition option on the table for later, once there is dedicated capital and management bandwidth for it post-raise, rather than forcing it into a window where the priority is proving organic, clean performance.

Symplast itself remains a reasonable acquisition candidate for a later phase — self-funded, right-sized, no competing institutional owner to negotiate around (see its dedicated profile) — but it is deliberately not part of the current path forward.

## Sequencing the Work

**Near-term (roughly the first two quarters): build the measurement infrastructure.** None of the five metrics above can be improved deliberately if they aren't being tracked with the right granularity today, and a real screening process will ask for a breakdown by customer segment and time period, not a single blended number. This means standing up monthly reporting on the software-versus-services revenue split, cohort-based retention and churn (segmented by practice size and by how long a customer has been on the platform, and separately comparing performance before and after the October 2024 leadership change), and a customer acquisition cost figure that's tracked as its own line item rather than buried in a blended marketing-spend number. This is unglamorous, internally-facing work, but it's the precondition for everything that follows — both because it's how progress gets measured and because a growth equity investor's own diligence team will ask for exactly this data in exactly this format.

**Middle stretch (extending through roughly the first year to fourteen months, overlapping the measurement work): execute the four levers above.** This is where the actual movement on the metrics happens — proving the AI product's impact, rebalancing service delivery, tightening the sales-to-onboarding motion, and establishing the EMR/EHR partnership conversation. Realistically this doesn't happen all at once or in a strict sequence; the AI-impact data collection in particular needs to start early because it takes many months of before-and-after comparison to become credible.

**Later stretch (roughly months fourteen through twenty): build the external narrative.** This is the smaller, market-facing work-stream — engaging an advisor with relevant experience in vertical software and specifically growth equity relationships (as distinct from buyout-oriented private equity, which is a different kind of relationship and a different pitch), assembling a deliberate list of the right categories of investor, and preparing clear, evidenced answers to the questions any serious conversation will raise — chief among them how the company competes simultaneously against point-solution software vendors and against traditional marketing agencies, and, now that clinical charting is being addressed through partnership rather than acquisition, why that's the right call rather than a gap.

**Final stretch (roughly months twenty through twenty-four): run an actual process.** With the metrics bar cleared and real data behind the product story, this becomes a competitive process among multiple interested parties rather than a single negotiation, which is where real leverage on price and terms comes from. This is also the natural point to give the existing search-fund investor group liquidity — a well-structured growth equity transaction commonly combines new primary capital for the business with a secondary component that lets some or all of the existing holders sell part of their position, and the straightforward ownership structure already in place (a single class of preferred stock held on the same terms across the search-fund investors, a modest pool of common stock held by a small number of individuals, and incentive equity reserved for the leadership team) means this should be a matter of scheduling and coordination rather than negotiation.

## Where This Could Go Wrong

**The growth and margin targets can pull against each other.** Mid-teens revenue growth generally requires continued investment in sales and marketing; a 10-to-15% EBITDA margin requires discipline on spending. Funding both simultaneously — enough growth investment to hit the top-line target while still showing profitability — is a genuine balancing act, and it's easy to over-index on one at the expense of the other depending on which metric gets more attention in a given quarter.

**Automating service delivery and improving retention can also pull against each other**, as noted above. If the push toward a 75%-plus gross margin isn't segmented carefully, it risks eroding the service quality that keeps smaller, price-sensitive practices from churning — directly undermining the retention target in the course of chasing the margin target.

**The customer base is inherently more volatile than enterprise software.** Single-location, owner-operated med spas are small businesses, and small businesses close, change hands, or simply stop investing in growth for reasons that have nothing to do with product quality — a slow economic environment for elective, discretionary spending is a real risk to both the growth and retention targets that isn't fully within the company's control. The broader research in this vault also notes that the paid-advertising channels much of the industry relies on for lead generation appear to be getting more competitive and more expensive over time, which pressures the sales-efficiency metric from the outside.

**Declining the clinical-charting acquisition doesn't make the underlying competitive gap disappear.** Multiple competitors in the space are able to point to real electronic medical record functionality that Growth99 doesn't have. A partnership or integration approach can blunt that objection, but it requires actually executing on that partnership — identifying the right EMR/EHR provider, building the integration, and making it a credible part of the pitch — rather than simply removing the acquisition from the plan and hoping the topic doesn't come up.

**Measurement maturity is itself a project, not a given.** Several of the metrics above likely aren't tracked today at the segmentation level a serious investor conversation requires. Building that reporting infrastructure — coordinating finance, sales operations, and product teams around a shared, credible set of definitions — takes real calendar time and competes directly for the same organizational bandwidth as the actual performance-improvement work.

**Coordinating the existing investor group, even in a genuinely simple structure, still takes real time.** A clean cap table removes the risk of a contentious negotiation over consent rights or liquidation preferences, but it doesn't eliminate the practical work of coordinating a board process, aligning a multi-party investor group on timing, and running the transaction through counsel — that's calendar time worth planning for even though it isn't expected to be a source of friction.

## Immediate Next Steps

The highest-leverage starting point is standing up the measurement infrastructure described above — the current baseline against each of the five metrics, tracked with the segmentation a real conversation will require, updated on a recurring cadence rather than produced once retroactively. In parallel, initial groundwork on the artificial-intelligence-impact data collection should begin now, given how many months of before-and-after comparison it takes to become a credible data point. The clinical-charting partnership conversation can also begin on a low-key, exploratory basis without committing significant resources, simply to understand what a realistic integration path would look like when the time comes to have that conversation with prospective investors.
