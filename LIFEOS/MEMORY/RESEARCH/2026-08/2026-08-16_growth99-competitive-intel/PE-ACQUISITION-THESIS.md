# Exit Readiness Plan — Positioning Growth99 for a Growth Equity Investment in 12–24 Months

**Reframed pass 2, 2026-08-16; refined pass 3.** The original version of this document (pass 1) analyzed Growth99 from a PE **buyer's** diligence perspective. Pass 2 reoriented every finding toward the opposite goal: **getting Growth99 acquired by a PE firm within 12–24 months**, at the best achievable multiple. Pass 3 sharpened the target further: the realistic path runs through a **growth equity investor** specifically — later-stage growth capital (often paired with partial secondary liquidity for existing holders) rather than a full LBO-style buyout — and added a concrete metrics bar to clear before that conversation can even start. This revision resolves the cap-table question: the structure is simple and clean, which removes what had looked like the plan's second-biggest risk. Facts are the same underlying research; the roadmap is strategic judgment.

## BLUF

Growth99's path to a growth equity investment is roughly **80% execution against the current transformation plan and 20% process mechanics** — building the story, cleaning up the public profile, and identifying the right investor landscape. The bridge between those two is getting clarity on, and then hitting, a specific **foundational metrics profile**: a growth equity investor's initial screen is a distinct, earlier hurdle than the "unique story and valuable IP" pitch, and the company doesn't clear it automatically just by having a good story. Roughly: **mid-teens revenue growth**, **solid sales economics** (CAC/payback that holds up), **>75% gross margin**, **10–15% EBITDA margin** (exact target depends on how much growth capital is layered in), and **reasonable SMB churn/NRR**. None of these are exotic for a vertical SaaS platform at this scale — they're "clear the bar" thresholds, not stretch goals — and because Growth99 already has real scale (1,000+ practices), **small improvements in sales conversion or churn compound unusually fast across a book this size.** Line up two or three of these simultaneously and the momentum becomes obvious rather than something that has to be argued for.

**Two things that might have looked like structural risk are actually non-issues.** The cap table is clean: a single class of preferred stock across all search-fund investors at the same price and terms, a small common pool held by a few individuals, and incentive units for leadership — a structure like that clears board approval for a transaction with essentially no hurdles, and the multi-entity investor count reflects a broad syndicate, not a layered or contentious stack. And founder **Cameron Hemphill's exit is confirmed clean** — COO at an unrelated firm as of March 2026, no lingering claim or overhang. That leaves the real work concentrated in two places: **hitting the metrics profile** above, and **closing the EMR/clinical-charting gap** via **Symplast**, the highest-leverage story move available.

## The foundational metrics profile — the bridge between execution and story

This is the specific bar a growth equity investor's screen is checking against before anyone engages with the differentiated-IP narrative. Treat it as the primary Phase 1 deliverable, not a byproduct of "getting the numbers clean":

| Metric | Target | Why it's the bar, not a stretch goal |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | Mid-teens (roughly 13–17%), sustained | A single strong quarter doesn't clear a screen; a trend line does |
| Sales economics | "Solid," not exceptional | CAC and payback period need to hold up under scrutiny, not lead the category |
| Gross margin | >75% | The line that separates a software story from an agency story — see the SaaS-vs-services split question in [Companies/Growth99.md](Companies/Growth99.md) |
| EBITDA margin | 10–15% | Exact target flexes with how much growth capital is layered into the deal |
| Churn / NRR | "Reasonable" for an SMB-heavy book | Benchmarked against SMB peers, not enterprise SaaS — see the churn-risk framing in [LANDSCAPE.md](LANDSCAPE.md) |

The operating-leverage point worth internalizing: at Growth99's existing scale, small improvements in sales conversion or churn don't add up linearly — they compound across the installed base fast enough that hitting two or three of these targets at once is more realistic than it sounds, and tends to make the rest of the profile click into place rather than requiring five separate independent pushes.

## The cap table — clean, and not a workstream

Worth stating plainly since it changes the shape of Phase 1: the cap table does **not** need a cleanup project. The structure is a single class of preferred stock held across all search-fund investors at the same price and terms, a small common pool held by a few individuals, and incentive units reserved for leadership — genuinely simple, not a layered stack with competing protective provisions. A structure like that is expected to clear board approval for a transaction with no real hurdles. (Public-record research separately confirmed no SEC Form D trail exists for "Growth99," "Growth99 Holdings," or "Pracinco" — consistent with, not contradictory to, a straightforward private stock purchase rather than a registered offering.) The practical upshot: none of Phase 1's effort needs to go toward cap-table consolidation — it can all go toward the metrics profile instead.

## The 12–24 month roadmap

### Phase 1 (Months 0–6): Move the numbers to the target profile
The single biggest risk to a growth equity conversation isn't a bad number — it's an unprovable one, or a provable one that misses the bar above. A screen doing what this vault's research just did will hit the same wall on financials specifically: no public revenue mix, no public NRR/churn. That's normal for a private company, but it means the *first* ask in a real conversation is a clean data room, and hitting the target profile takes real internal work, not a document dump. With the cap table already clean (see above), this phase can concentrate entirely on the numbers.
- **Instrument and report the SaaS-vs-managed-services split** by dollar and by margin, monthly, starting now — so 12–24 months from now there's a trend line toward >75% gross margin, not a single retroactive estimate.
- **Build NRR/gross-churn cohorts segmented by practice size and tenure**, and separately, **pre- vs. post-October-2024-CEO-transition cohorts.** This is also how "reasonable SMB churn/NRR" gets proven rather than asserted.
- **Track sales economics (CAC/payback) as its own line**, not folded into blended revenue reporting — this is the "solid sales economics" leg of the profile and the one most likely to be underinstrumented today.
- **No action needed on Hemphill or the cap table.** Both confirmed clean — see [People/Cameron-Hemphill.md](People/Cameron-Hemphill.md) and the cap-table note above.

### Phase 2 (Months 4–14, overlapping Phase 1): Close the EMR gap and prove the AI story
This phase is the actual value-creation engine — the difference between a story that's "a marketing agency with software" and one that's "the platform of record for aesthetic practices." It's also where the mid-teens growth target gets earned rather than hoped for.
- **Pursue Symplast as an acquisition.** See [Companies/Symplast.md](Companies/Symplast.md) for the full profile. It is the strongest bolt-on candidate found: specialty-matched (cosmetic surgery/med spa EMR), realistically sized (~$8M ARR range against Growth99's own ~$25–50M estimated revenue), and — critically — has no competing institutional PE owner to negotiate around, unlike Meevo (Serent Capital-backed) or Nextech (TPG-owned). There is no evidence Symplast is currently for sale; this requires proactive, direct outreach, not waiting for a process.
- **Get real, measurable data on Gia's impact — not marketing copy.** Every public claim about Gia's retention/conversion impact traces back to Growth99's own marketing (see [Products/Gia-AI-Front-Desk.md](Products/Gia-AI-Front-Desk.md)). Twelve to eighteen months of real before/after cohort data — response time, conversion rate, retention lift, ideally per-practice — converts "marketing language" into a defensible part of both the growth-rate story and the churn/NRR leg of the metrics profile.
- **Use the AI investment to cut services COGS, not just to pitch customers.** Applying the same technology to reduce the labor cost of account-manager-dependent SEO/ad-management delivery is a direct, provable lever on the gross-margin target — arguably more convincing than the customer-facing pitch, because it shows up in the margin line, not just marketing copy.

### Phase 3 (Months 14–20): Build the equity story and engage the market
This is the 20%, and it's deliberately the lighter lift once Phases 1–2 have done their work — the story doesn't have to carry the deal on its own if the metrics profile is already in place.
- **Engage a sell-side advisor with vertical-SaaS or healthcare-martech experience**, and specifically growth equity relationships (as distinct from buyout-focused PE), 6–9 months ahead of a target close, once Phases 1–2 have real data behind them. Going to market before the profile is provable risks anchoring investors to an agency-multiple read that's hard to walk back later in the same process.
- **Build the investor list deliberately**, weighted toward growth equity funds with vertical-SaaS or healthcare-software theses, plus: (a) larger practice-management/EHR platforms looking to add marketing-automation capability (the Tebra/PatientPop precedent in this vault is a cautionary tale on post-merger integration quality, not a reason to avoid this category — see [Companies/Tebra-PatientPop.md](Companies/Tebra-PatientPop.md)), and (b) horizontal marketing-automation platforms seeking a healthcare vertical beachhead.
- **Pre-empt the two-fronts question.** Any investor will ask "how do you win against point-solution CRMs and against agencies simultaneously" (see [LANDSCAPE.md](LANDSCAPE.md)) — have a crisp, evidenced answer ready rather than fielding it live for the first time in a management presentation.

### Phase 4 (Months 20–24): Run the process
- With the metrics profile hit, a closed EMR acquisition, and real AI-impact data in hand, run a competitive process rather than a single-bidder negotiation.
- Give the existing search-fund investors (Pracinco, Trilogy, Hunter Search Capital, and the smaller LPs) liquidity alongside new growth capital coming in — a well-sequenced growth equity deal commonly combines primary growth capital with a secondary component for exactly this kind of existing syndicate. With a single-class, same-terms cap table already in place, board approval is expected to be a formality rather than a negotiation.

## What could blow the 12–24 month timeline

- **If the metrics profile isn't hit** — growth stays single-digit, gross margin stays services-diluted, or churn runs hot — the conversation doesn't fail outright, but it downgrades from "obvious momentum" to a harder sell requiring the story to do more work than it realistically can. Track the five metrics quarterly against the targets above, not just at the end of Phase 1.
- **If Symplast (or an equivalent target) isn't willing to sell**, the EMR gap has to be built organically instead — slower, less certain inside 24 months, and a drag on the growth-rate leg of the profile too. Worth quietly scoping a build-vs-buy fallback in Phase 1, not after a Symplast approach fails.
