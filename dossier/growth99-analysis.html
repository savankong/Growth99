Exit Readiness Plan

A working plan for positioning Growth99 for a growth equity investment within 12–24 months: later-stage growth capital, typically combined with some liquidity for existing shareholders, as distinct from a full leveraged buyout (LBO). Revision 4 expands the plan substantially and removes a near-term clinical-software acquisition from the path forward — that route is deprioritized for now, not abandoned. Analytical judgment layered on researched facts; confidence tags on individual factual claims trace back to public research.

The big picture

Two work-streams have to converge for Growth99 to be an attractive growth equity candidate. The larger one, by far, is simply running the business well enough that the numbers tell a compelling story on their own — revenue expanding at a healthy clip, margins that look like software rather than an agency, and a customer base that sticks around. The smaller one is packaging that story for an outside audience: cleaning up the public profile, understanding which categories of investor fit, and having answers ready for the questions a screening process will raise. Neither substitutes for the other, but the balance matters — most of the near-term effort belongs on the operating fundamentals, with the market-facing work picked up later as a comparatively contained project once those fundamentals are in hand.

The connective tissue between the two is a specific, checkable bar of financial metrics. A growth equity investor's first pass at a company is less about the qualitative story and more a screen against a fairly standard profile — a business can have a genuinely differentiated product and still not get a second meeting if it doesn't clear that initial bar. Because Growth99 already has real scale (over a thousand practices on the platform), the business has meaningful operating leverage: a modest improvement in either the win rate on new sales or the retention rate on existing customers ripples through the recurring-revenue base and moves several metrics at once, rather than requiring five separate, unrelated initiatives.

None of these are unusual for a vertical software business of Growth99's size — they read as baseline expectations for the category, not stretch goals.

Path forward without a near-term clinical software acquisition

Earlier versions of this plan treated acquiring a small electronic medical record (EMR) / electronic health record (EHR) company — specifically Symplast, profiled below — as the centerpiece of the value-creation story. That's set aside for now: an acquisition introduces integration risk, diverts management bandwidth, and plausibly depresses the EBITDA margin target in the near term, all at the moment the priority is showing clean, organically-improving numbers. The alternative leans on four levers that don't require deal capital or integration risk.

1 · Make the AI product's impact provable

Gia's retention/conversion claims are currently marketing language, not data. Quantified before/after cohort data (response time, booking conversion, retention lift, by practice) does double duty: it becomes a defensible part of the growth story, and applying the same technology internally to cut manual account-management labor is a direct, provable lever on the gross-margin target.

2 · Rebalance service delivery by segment, not uniformly

Cutting service labor indiscriminately to hit the margin target risks pushing churn the wrong way. Heavier automation for smaller/lower-tier accounts where white-glove service doesn't pencil out; preserved or increased high-touch service for larger accounts and at-risk renewals where the retention economics justify it.

3 · Link sales efficiency and early-tenure churn

Churn in a small-practice customer base likely concentrates in the first few months — customers mis-sold, under-onboarded, or never shown enough value. Tighter sales qualification and a more deliberate onboarding motion can move both the acquisition-efficiency and retention metrics together.

4 · Answer the clinical-charting gap with partnership, not ownership

The gap is real and competitors point to it (see EMR / clinical gap). A lighter-weight integration or referral partnership with an existing EMR/EHR provider addresses the objection without the capital outlay or margin drag of ownership — keeping acquisition on the table for later, once there's dedicated capital and bandwidth post-raise.

Symplast remains a reasonable candidate for a later phase — self-funded, right-sized, no competing institutional owner (see its profile below) — but it is deliberately not part of the current path.

Sequencing the work

Near-term · ~first 2 quarters

Build the measurement infrastructure

None of the five metrics can be improved deliberately without tracking them at the right granularity today. Stand up monthly software-vs-services revenue reporting, cohort-based retention/churn (by practice size, tenure, and pre/post the October 2024 leadership change), and CAC as its own tracked line — the precondition for everything else, and what a real screening process will ask for regardless.

Middle stretch · ~months 2–14

Execute the four levers

Prove the AI product's impact, rebalance service delivery, tighten the sales-to-onboarding motion, and open the EMR/EHR partnership conversation. The AI-impact data collection needs to start early — it takes many months of before/after comparison to become credible.

Later stretch · ~months 14–20

Build the external narrative

Engage an advisor with vertical-software and specifically growth equity relationships (distinct from buyout-oriented private equity). Build a deliberate investor list. Prepare evidenced answers to the two-fronts competitive question and to why the EMR/EHR gap is being addressed via partnership rather than acquisition.

Final stretch · ~months 20–24

Run an actual process

With the metrics bar cleared and real product data in hand, run a competitive process among multiple parties rather than a single negotiation. Give the existing search-fund investor group liquidity alongside new primary capital — the straightforward ownership structure already in place should make this a matter of scheduling, not negotiation.

Where this could go wrong

Growth vs. margin. Mid-teens growth generally requires continued sales/marketing investment; a 10–15% EBITDA margin requires spending discipline. Funding both at once is a genuine balancing act.
Automation vs. retention. Pushing gross margin toward 75%+ without careful segmentation risks eroding the service quality that keeps price-sensitive practices from churning — directly undermining the retention target while chasing the margin one.
Customer-base volatility. Single-location, owner-operated med spas are small businesses that close or stop investing for reasons unrelated to product quality; a soft environment for discretionary spending is a real risk outside the company's control, compounded by rising paid-advertising costs across the category (see The Landscape).
The competitive gap doesn't disappear. Declining the acquisition doesn't remove the fact that named competitors have real clinical-charting functionality Growth99 lacks. A partnership approach can blunt the objection, but only if it's actually executed, not just implied.
Measurement maturity is its own project. Several target metrics likely aren't tracked today at the segmentation a serious investor conversation needs. Building that reporting infrastructure takes real time from finance, sales operations, and product — competing directly with the improvement work itself.
Coordination still takes time. A clean cap table removes negotiation risk, not calendar time — aligning a multi-party investor group and running a transaction through counsel is real work worth planning for.

Immediate next steps

The highest-leverage starting point is the measurement infrastructure above — a current baseline against all five metrics, tracked with real segmentation, updated on a recurring cadence rather than produced once retroactively. In parallel, artificial-intelligence-impact data collection should begin now, given how long it takes to become credible. The clinical-charting partnership conversation can also start on a low-key, exploratory basis, simply to understand what a realistic integration path looks like before it's needed in an investor conversation.

Financial Metrics & Tracking Framework

The five headline targets above are what a growth equity screen checks first. This is the fuller set of financial and operating metrics a hybrid software-and-services business like Growth99 should track on an ongoing basis — both because real diligence goes several layers deeper, and because these are the numbers that let the business manage toward the targets, not just report on them afterward. Every acronym is defined at first use.

1 · Growth & revenue

Annual/monthly recurring revenue (ARR/MRR), split between platform revenue and managed-services revenue — a blended total hides the mix question that drives the valuation multiple. The ARR bridge (or waterfall): new-business ARR, expansion ARR, contraction ARR, and churned ARR reconciled for the period — more diagnostic than any single growth number, since it shows whether growth comes from new logos, the existing base, or both. Bookings vs. recognized revenue wherever contracts involve prepay.

2 · Retention & churn

Gross revenue retention (GRR) — revenue retained excluding expansion, the "floor" that can't exceed 100% — tracked separately from net revenue retention (NRR), which adds expansion back in. The two are often conflated but tell different stories. Logo churn vs. revenue churn (likely to diverge given the small-practice customer base). Cohort retention curves by signup vintage — the most direct way to test whether the post-2024 transition is actually improving retention. Churn reason coding: voluntary vs. involuntary (failed payment, practice closure).

3 · Unit economics & sales efficiency

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) by channel, and CAC payback period. Customer lifetime value (LTV) and the LTV:CAC ratio (healthy SaaS benchmark: roughly 3:1 or better). The "Magic Number" — net new ARR in a quarter divided by the prior quarter's sales & marketing spend — a quick efficiency read. Sales cycle length, win rate, and average contract value (ACV) as the underlying drivers.

4 · Margin & profitability

Gross margin split by revenue line (platform vs. services) rather than blended — the number that most directly answers the "software or agency" question. Contribution margin by product/tier. EBITDA margin alongside its components (sales & marketing, product/R&D, G&A, each as % of revenue). The "Rule of 40" — growth rate plus profit margin should sum to roughly 40%+ — combines the growth and profitability targets into one check.

5 · Customer health & concentration

Customer count and growth by segment/tier. Revenue concentration — % from the largest customers or any single multi-location group (flagged as an open question elsewhere in this vault). Customer satisfaction signals (NPS, support ticket volume/resolution time) as leading indicators that move before churn does. Product usage/feature adoption, especially for the AI product — usage depth typically predicts retention better than revenue alone.

6 · Cash & working capital

Cash balance and runway — lower priority if already profitable, still worth tracking through any investment period. Deferred revenue/billings as a leading indicator of forward bookings. Days sales outstanding (DSO) — rising DSO can signal customer financial distress before it shows up as churn, meaningful given a small-business customer base.

7 · Services-specific operational metrics. A meaningful share of the business is delivered through account managers and campaign staff, so standard software metrics alone won't capture the full picture: utilization rate (productive vs. idle staff time, a direct input to services gross margin), revenue per account manager (should trend up if the automation levers in the exit-readiness plan are working), and services gross margin tracked as its own line — arguably the single most important number in this framework, since it reveals whether the services side is a genuine profit center, a retention-driving loss-leader, or a drag needing restructuring.

How this connects to the exit-readiness plan

The five headline metrics are the subset a growth equity screen checks first. The rest of this framework is what makes those five numbers trustworthy rather than just asserted — cohort retention curves are what make an NRR figure credible, the gross-margin split by revenue line is what makes the target meaningful rather than a blended number hiding a weak mix, and the services-specific metrics are what make the margin-expansion levers in the exit readiness plan actually executable.

The Landscape

Growth99 sits inside the med spa / aesthetics / elective-wellness software market — every source found agrees it is growing fast, driven by injectables and aesthetic procedures becoming routine consumer spend. The reliable figures put the U.S. med-spa-software slice at roughly $119M (2023) growing toward ~$432M (2033), a 13.8% CAGR; a much larger figure ($950.5B) surfaced in one source and is flagged unreliable, likely a scoping error. Growth99 itself already claims 1,000+ practices and 2,300+ users — worth reconciling against real revenue once available.

Two competitive fronts

Point-solution software — Boulevard, Mangomint, PatientNow, RepeatMD, Vagaro, AestheticsPro, Tebra — none matched Growth99's top-of-funnel marketing depth, but several beat it on the capability it structurally lacks: clinical/EMR charting.

Traditional agencies — Rosemont Media, Etna Interactive, Brenton Way, Cardinal, Intrepy, Medstar, Influx, Studio 3 Marketing — identified but not yet profiled (see Method). Growth99's own marketing pitches directly against this front: "Better Than A Med Spa Digital Marketing Agency."

Unlike a typical VC-backed SaaS competitor set, Growth99 is not founder-led anymore. Ownership structure looked like it might be the largest risk factor in any new investment — instead, both major ownership questions have resolved cleanly: the cap table is a simple single-class structure expected to clear board approval without friction, and founder Cameron Hemphill's exit is fully separated. The real work is on the operating side. See Ownership & Leadership.

The search-fund ownership model

Trend

Growth99 is a live example: acquired in 2024 via a traditional search fund, with Pickell backed by six-plus named investor entities rather than a single lead. Structurally distinct from VC/growth-equity SaaS — search funds acquire an already-profitable operating business and install an outside operator-CEO. Despite the entity count, the resulting cap table here is understood to be simple — a single class of preferred at the same price/terms across the syndicate, a small common pool, and leadership incentive units — not the layered stack a large investor count might otherwise suggest.

Also bears on the primary-vs-secondary question: search-fund deals commonly give the operating entrepreneur founder-level equity while existing search-fund investors keep preferred positions — a well-sequenced growth equity deal would likely combine new primary capital with a secondary component giving this syndicate liquidity.
